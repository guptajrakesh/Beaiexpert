Let's start with a problem. Imagine asking an AI assistant a question about your own company. It answers instantly, and it sounds confident. But the answer is wrong. Why? The model was never trained on your company's private documents. And its knowledge is frozen at a training cutoff date, so it can't know anything newer. When a model lacks information, it doesn't stay silent. It produces a plausible guess.

One idea is to paste all your documents into every prompt. That works for a few pages. But a real company has thousands. They won't fit in the model's context window. And even when they do... ...every question becomes slow and expensive, and the important detail gets lost in the noise.
A better way to think about it
📕

Closed-book exam

Answer from memory.
Might misremember — or make something up.

📖

Open-book exam

Look up the right page first, then answer.
Accurate — and you can cite the source.

RAG = giving the AI an open-book exam

Here's a better way to think about it. A plain language model is like a student taking a closed-book exam: it answers from memory. RAG turns it into an open-book exam. First look up the relevant pages, then answer using them. Retrieval-Augmented Generation: retrieve the right information, then generate the answer.
The name says it all
🔍

Retrieval

Search your documents for the passages that matter.

➕

Augmented

Add those passages to the prompt.

✍️

Generation

The LLM writes an answer grounded in them.

The name describes the three steps. Retrieval: search your documents for the passages that matter for this question. Augmented: add those passages to the prompt. Generation: the language model writes an answer grounded in them.

A RAG system works in two phases. The first is indexing, which you do once, ahead of time. Start with your documents: PDFs, web pages, policies, notes. Split them into smaller passages, called chunks, so we can find precise pieces later. Turn each chunk into an embedding: a list of numbers that captures its meaning. Store those embeddings in an index, ready to be searched.

Why embeddings? Picture every chunk as a point on a map of meaning. Chunks about similar topics end up close together. When a question arrives, we turn it into a point on the same map... ...and the closest chunks are the most relevant, even if they use completely different words. That's semantic search, and it's the heart of retrieval.

Phase two happens every time someone asks a question. We embed the question with the same model, and find the closest chunks in the index. Then we build a prompt: clear instructions, the retrieved passages, and the question. Notice the instructions: use only this context, and say "I don't know" if the answer isn't there. Finally, the language model reads it all and writes the answer.

Let's ask the same question again, this time with RAG. The answer is correct, because it comes from the actual policy document. And it cites its sources, so anyone can check where the answer came from. That's the difference between a confident guess and a grounded answer.

When should you use RAG?

  • ✓ Answers live in private or internal documents
  • ✓ Information changes often
  • ✓ Far too much to fit in a prompt
  • ✓ Answers must cite sources people can verify
💬 Customer support
🏢 Internal knowledge bases
⚖️ Policy & legal Q&A
🔎 Document search
So when should you use RAG? When the answers live in private or internal documents... ...when information changes often, or there's far too much to fit in a prompt... ...and whenever answers need sources people can verify. That's why RAG powers so many real assistants: customer support, internal knowledge bases, and document search.
